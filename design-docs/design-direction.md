# Design direction

This is a point of view, not a spec. It's here so anyone in LOGICA (the board, a new
contributor, whoever picks this up next) can look at the landing page and explain why
it looks the way it does, without having been in the conversations behind it.

Rules for building live in [style-guide.md](style-guide.md). The landing page layout
lives in [landing-page.md](landing-page.md). Open questions are in the
[README](README.md).

## 1. The concept

LOGICA's mark already says what the org is: a circuit that branches like a tree,
growing inside a circle. Logic and structure on one side, organic growth and culture
on the other, and neither one wins. The landing page should be that same idea at full
scale.

That rules out two easy versions: a tech-org site with some Latin American color
thrown on top, and a "cultural" site that happens to teach CS. The site should be
exact, structured and built on a visible grid, and also clearly and specifically
Latinx, with its color and pattern logic taken from real art instead of a mood board.

The feeling we're after: **this is ours, it's exact, it's excellent, and you'd be
lucky to be part of it.** Exciting and a little exclusive. A specific, confident thing
that then makes the effort to welcome you in, rather than a generic "everyone's
welcome here" banner.

## 2. Cultural references, by country

Each of these is a real piece or tradition, picked for one concrete thing it gives us:
a color, a structural idea, a way of moving. None of them should be reproduced or
imitated directly. They're the reasoning behind the palette and type in sections 4
and 5, not source material to trace.

### Uruguay: Joaquín Torres-García, Constructive Universalism (1930s)

Torres-García built paintings as strict grids. He divided the canvas into cells with
orthogonal lines, then filled each cell with a primary color or a pictograph (a ship,
a clock, a fish). It's the closest existing precedent for logic and art in one frame:
a rigorous grid that still carries warmth and symbols instead of reading as cold
minimalism.

**What it gives us:** how a grid-based layout can hold meaning instead of just
organizing content.

### Venezuela: Carlos Cruz-Diez, the *Physichromie* series (1959 to 2019)

Cruz-Diez made color that changes as you walk past it. Thin vertical bands of
saturated color are arranged so your eye mixes them into hues that aren't printed
anywhere on the surface. It's kinetic art made entirely from color interaction, with
no moving parts.

**What it gives us:** color and motion treated as one design problem. A scroll or
hover driven color shift is a culturally grounded move here, not just a trend.

### Mexico: Diego Rivera's murals and Frida Kahlo's Casa Azul

Rivera's murals (*Detroit Industry*, the Ministry of Public Education panels) put
bold, saturated, large figures directly into public buildings. They're meant to be
understood while walking past, not studied in a gallery. Kahlo's house, La Casa Azul,
is painted a saturated cobalt blue on the outside, loud enough to be a landmark.

**What it gives us:** permission for the hero to be big, immediate and saturated
rather than restrained, plus the cobalt blue itself (see section 4).

### Mexico: Talavera de Puebla ceramics

A colonial-era craft that's still made by hand today: a cream ceramic ground carrying
repeating cobalt, marigold-yellow and green motifs in a tightly bounded pattern
grammar.

**What it gives us:** a warm cream ground instead of white, carrying a disciplined,
repeating accent pattern. Useful for section dividers or background texture that
doesn't compete with content.

### Panama: Guna mola textiles

Reverse-appliqué panels made from several layers of contrasting cloth, cut and
stitched back to reveal the layer underneath. They're geometric or figurative (birds,
fish, plants) and always in saturated red, yellow, black and blue. Each mola is one
artisan's own variation on a shared visual grammar.

**What it gives us:** "structured personalization." A repeating system, like a card
grid or an icon set, where every instance is visibly considered rather than stamped
out from one template.

### Colombia and Venezuela: Wayuu mochila weaving

Tapestry-crocheted bags made by Wayuu artisans of the Guajira Peninsula. The
geometric pattern is literally a story the weaver is telling: animals, plants,
personal symbols, worked in bright, high-contrast color.

**What it gives us:** pattern as narrative. Decorative elements on the site
(dividers, icon treatments) come from one consistent geometric vocabulary instead of
arbitrary decoration.

### Bolivia and the wider Andes (Peru, Ecuador): aguayo weaving

A woven cloth with a clear structure: a bordered edge (*t'irja*), a solid-color field
(*pampa*) and a patterned band (*salta*), made from magenta, red and green wool, often
naturally dyed.

**What it gives us:** a three-part composition (border, field, pattern band) that
maps onto how a section of a web page is actually built. It's also a good guide to how
much decoration versus open space a section should carry.

### Brazil: Neo-Concretism (Hélio Oiticica, Lygia Clark, late 1950s to 1960s)

A Rio-based break from strict geometric Concrete art. The argument was that color and
form should be felt and participated in, not only looked at: Oiticica's hanging color
constructions, Clark's *Bichos* sculptures that you move with your hands.

**What it gives us:** interactivity as a form of warmth, not just a UX nicety. An
element you can nudge, or that responds to you, does the same job Neo-Concretism gave
to participation fifty years ago.

### Cuba: Wifredo Lam, *The Jungle* (1943)

Lam fused Cubism and Surrealism with Afro-Cuban religious imagery and the visual
density of Cuban sugarcane fields: crescent-shaped masks, dense vertical forms, deep
greens, blues and purples.

**What it gives us:** the deep green used as a secondary accent (see section 4), and a
reminder that Latin American art already includes Afro-Caribbean and Indigenous
traditions, not only the muralist mainstream. It's part of why the reference set
can't stop at Mexico.

## 3. The community spotlight

The site should make members feel like they're standing next to something real,
without inventing anything to create that feeling. Two things do that honestly, and
one rule keeps it honest.

**Featured Content is the spotlight.** `CONTENT.md` already asks for "Featured LOGICA
announcements, projects, or community content" with a title, description and link. We
use that slot for real LOGICA people and real outcomes: a member's internship, a
hackathon placement, a shipped project, a talk, with a name, a photo and a link to
their profile page (`CONTENT.md` section 4). Real people and specific results from the
org itself, not copy written to sound like a testimonial.

**Aim high without name-dropping.** The brief's reference points (a Rick Owens-level
aesthetic point of view, Latinx people who've reached major roles in modern life,
Duolingo's willingness to be a specific, weird brand) aren't names to put on the
page. One of them is worth knowing about, though, because it matches the brief
exactly: Luis von Ahn, the Guatemalan computer scientist who invented CAPTCHA and
reCAPTCHA and co-founded Duolingo, is the same person who gave Duolingo the
confidence to be an unmistakable brand instead of a generic edtech product. The
brief's own comparison point already has a Latinx computer scientist inside it.
Naming him or peers like him (Ellen Ochoa, the first Latina astronaut; Sonia
Sotomayor; France Córdova, the first Latina to direct the National Science
Foundation) isn't required, but it shows the kind of achievement the spotlight should
reach for: specific, technical and real.

**No invented numbers, ever.** If LOGICA can't honestly point to "500+ members placed
at top companies," the page doesn't say it. One named, linked, real story is worth
more than any stat block, and it's the only version the hard-ban list allows.

## 4. Palette

Every color here came from a specific reference in section 2. None was picked first
and justified afterward. Two colors have a documented hex: Rosa Mexicano and cobalt
blue. The others come from handmade, natural-dye crafts (Talavera, Wayuu, aguayo) that
don't have one official spec, so their hex values are representative, sampled from
commonly reproduced photographs of the real objects.

| Swatch | Hex | Source | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rosa Mexicano | `#E4007C` | Named and popularized by Mexican designer Ramón Valdiosera in 1949, rooted in pre-Hispanic textile pink and bougainvillea. This is its standard documented hex. | **Primary.** The loudest color on the page, used sparingly (CTA button, key accents) |
| Cobalt Blue | `#0047AB` | The standard pigment hex for cobalt blue, the color of Talavera de Puebla glaze and Frida Kahlo's Casa Azul walls. | **Primary.** The main structural color: links, headings on light backgrounds, dark-mode surfaces |
| Cempasúchil Yellow | `#F2A900` | Sampled from the marigold (cempasúchil) accent glaze in Talavera ceramics and the yellow appliqué layer in Guna molas. | **Secondary accent.** Small, high-energy touches (icons, tags, hover states). Never text on a light background (see below) |
| Jungle Green | `#1B4D3E` | Sampled from the dense greens in Wifredo Lam's *The Jungle* (1943). | **Secondary.** A second safe text and link color next to cobalt, for variety in dense content |
| Ink | `#12100E` | A warm near-black, closer to the dark outlines in Rivera's murals than to a default UI black. | **Neutral.** Primary text, dark backgrounds |
| Paper | `#F5EDE1` | A warm cream, matched to the ceramic ground of Talavera pottery rather than a cold white. | **Neutral.** Primary background |

Rosa and cobalt are the primary pair, used together but not evenly. Cobalt carries
most of the page (text, structure). Rosa is saved for the few moments that should feel
loud, like the call to action or a key headline word. Cempasúchil and Jungle Green are
support, not a third and fourth equal color. The brief's own rule still holds: don't
use all of them at once on one screen.

### Contrast

WCAG AA needs 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large text and UI elements. Checked
against both neutrals:

- Cobalt on Paper: **7.3:1**. Passes for body text.
- Cobalt on white: **8.4:1**. Passes for body text.
- Jungle Green on Paper: **8.3:1**. Passes for body text.
- Ink on Paper: **16.4:1**. The default body-text pairing.
- Rosa on white: **4.6:1**. Passes AA, but only just. Fine for large or bold text and
  buttons. Don't set small body copy in Rosa.
- Rosa on Paper: **3.9:1**. Fails for body text, passes for large text and UI
  components. **Never use Rosa for small text on the cream background.** Keep it for
  buttons, large headline words, and borders or icons where the 3:1 threshold applies.
- Cempasúchil on white or Paper: **about 2:1 and 1.7:1**. Fails outright as text on
  either light neutral. **Cempasúchil is a fill and accent color only.** It works as a
  button or tag background with Ink text on top (9.4:1), never as text or fine detail
  on Paper or white.

The full list of approved pairings, including the dark-background ones, is in
[style-guide.md](style-guide.md#color).

## 5. Type

The idea is a display face with real Latin American sign-painting and lettering roots,
paired with a clean, very legible body face. It's the same "structure holds the
energy" logic as the palette.

**Sudtipos**, a type foundry founded in Buenos Aires by Alejandro Paul, specializes in
exactly this: typefaces drawn from Latin American commercial sign-painting, packaging
lettering and script traditions, with real technical polish (Type Directors Club
recognition, available on Adobe Fonts). A bold Sudtipos display face, used only for
headlines and wordmark-adjacent moments, gives the page a display voice that actually
comes from the tradition it's referencing, instead of a generic "ethnic-coded" display
font picked for vibes.

Everything else (body copy, UI labels, forms) uses a clean, neutral sans. The display
face should never have to be legible at 14px. That matches the brief: the contrast
between bold display and clean body is the point, and decoration should never hurt
readability.

Picking the exact families and clearing both for licensing and self-hosting under the
performance budget in `DESIGN.md` is still open. The current candidates are in
[style-guide.md](style-guide.md#type).

## 6. Voice and tone

LOGICA's own language, from its UIC organization page and LinkedIn listing, is short
and direct: *"Increasing the participation and success of students from Latinx and
underrepresented communities pursuing careers in the field of computing and computer
science,"* organized around three pillars: **diversity, growth and development, and
community.**

That's the voice to write toward. Plain, purposeful, first-person plural ("we," not
"LOGICA offers its members"), and never vague. The hero copy should answer in one
sentence what the mission statement answers: who this is for and what it does for
them. If a line of copy could be pasted onto another org's site without editing, it's
the wrong line.

## 7. Motion and interaction

This is about intent, not implementation. `DESIGN.md` already sets the technical bar:
CSS first, GSAP only for real orchestration, Lottie for vector moments, and a
`prefers-reduced-motion` fallback for every animation.

- Motion should work like Cruz-Diez's color: it shows a relationship (this leads to
  that, this is part of a set). Nothing moves just because motion is available.
- A scroll-driven reveal on the landing page tells LOGICA's story in order: who we
  are, what we do, how you get in. That's the same sequence `DESIGN.md` asks for.
- Interactive elements (hover, focus, something you can nudge) carry a little of
  Oiticica and Clark's "art you participate in": small responses that reward
  attention, not animation on static content.
- The mark is a strong candidate for one well-made load-in moment, the branching
  circuit tracing itself in, like a Netflix sting. That's a narrow piece of craft that
  needs its own spec later.

How each landing page section applies this direction is written up section by section
in [landing-page.md](landing-page.md).

## 8. What this is not

The hard-ban list, restated for this direction so nothing gets reinterpreted later:

- **No pill-shaped buttons.** The grid-based structure reads as rectangular and
  intentional: sharp or lightly rounded corners, never capsules.
- **No vague hero copy.** Section 6 exists to rule this out. The hero says who LOGICA
  is and what it does, in LOGICA's own words, not "empowering the next generation."
- **No fake reviews or testimonials.** The spotlight only ever shows real, named,
  linked members and real outcomes, never a quote written to sound authentic.
- **No purple gradients.** The palette is built from saturated, sourced hues (Mexican
  pink, cobalt, marigold, jungle green) instead.
- **No fake or invented metrics.** No stat block, no "500+ members," no made-up
  percentage. If a number can't be backed by a real source, it doesn't appear.
- **No AI-generated images**, and no "made with AI" badge or tag anywhere.
- **No em dashes in copy.**
- **No emoji as icons.** Icons are drawn, from the same geometric vocabulary as the
  patterns in section 2.

## 9. The hero, tested with AI image exploration

Section 7 left the mark's load-in as a narrow piece of craft for later. Three rounds
of AI-generated landing page mockups, used only to gut-check the direction, turned up
one finding worth writing down before it gets lost.

**The mark works as a literal object, not just an icon.** Shown as a physical, lit
sculpture (the branching circuit tree inside its circle, with metal or glass circuit
lines against a warm ceramic or bronze frame, closer to the Talavera and metalwork
references than to chrome), it reads as a centerpiece the hero can be built around,
more like a Netflix-sting object than a flat logo. That's worth treating as a real
direction for the eventual animated load-in.

**Material choice settles the exclusive versus welcoming tension from section 1,
without toning down either half.** The first dark hero used cold signals: stone
plinths, museum lighting, engraved inscriptions. It came across as an intimidating
institution, not an org someone wants to join. Swapping those for warm ones (soft
ambient light, natural materials, a lived-in room instead of a monument) while keeping
the sculptural mark and its cobalt glow as the one clear "precision and tech" anchor
gave a hero that felt exclusive and inviting at the same time. For whoever builds it:
dark and dramatic doesn't have to mean cold. Avoid literal stone, columns and
museum-plaza staging. Use warm materials and soft light.

**The Torres-García grid-cell pictograph works at small scale too.** Simple relief
icons made from the palette's flat color blocks are a good candidate for the Mission
blocks and similar small content units, instead of a generic line-icon set.

To be clear about what this was: image generation for a gut check, not asset
production. Nothing in those images is a real photo, a real icon or real copy. A 3D
artist or motion designer still has to build the object and its load-in, and every
generated face, name or headline in them is a placeholder for real members, real
photos and LOGICA's real voice.
