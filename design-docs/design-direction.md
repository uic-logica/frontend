# LOGICA Landing Page: Design Direction

This document is a point of view, not a spec. It exists so any LOGICA member (board, new contributor, whoever picks this up next) can look at the landing page and explain *why* it looks the way it does, in their own words, without having sat in on the conversations that produced it. It does not contain layouts, component names, or a style guide. Those come later, once this direction is reviewed.

## 1. The concept

LOGICA already tells you what it is in its own mark: a circuit that branches like a tree growing inside a circle. That's the whole idea, stated once, visually. Logic and structure on one hand, organic growth and culture on the other, neither one winning. The landing page should read as the same idea expanded to full scale. Not a tech-org site with some Latin American color thrown on top, and not a "cultural" site that happens to teach CS. One thing: exact, structured, grid-literate, and unmistakably, specifically Latinx, pulling real color and pattern logic from real art rather than a mood board of stock adjectives. The feeling to land on: **this is ours, it's exact, it's excellent, and you'd be lucky to be part of it.** Exciting and exclusive at once, not a generic "everyone's welcome here" banner, but a specific, confident thing that then makes the effort to actually welcome you in.

## 2. Cultural references, by country

Each of these is a real piece or tradition, chosen for one concrete thing it contributes: a color, a structural idea, a way of moving. None of these should be reproduced or imitated directly; they're the reasoning behind the choices in sections 4 and 5, not source material to trace.

**Uruguay. Joaquín Torres-García, Constructive Universalism (1930s).** Torres-García built paintings as strict grids, dividing the canvas into cells with orthogonal lines, then filling each cell with a primary color or a pictograph (a ship, a clock, a fish). It's the single closest existing precedent for "logic and art in one frame": a rigorous grid that still carries warmth and symbol instead of reading as cold minimalism. Contributes: the structural logic for how a grid-based layout can hold meaning instead of just organizing content.

**Venezuela. Carlos Cruz-Diez, the *Physichromie* series (1959 to 2019).** Cruz-Diez built color that changes as you move past it: thin vertical bands of saturated color, arranged so your eye mixes them into hues that aren't actually printed anywhere on the surface. It's kinetic art built entirely from color interaction, no moving parts. Contributes: the argument for treating color and motion as one design problem, not two. A scroll- or hover-driven color shift is a legitimate, culturally-grounded move here, not just a trend.

**Mexico. Muralism (Diego Rivera) and Frida Kahlo's Casa Azul.** Rivera's murals (*Detroit Industry*, the Ministry of Public Education panels) put bold, saturated, large-scale figures directly into public buildings: art meant to be walked past and understood immediately, not read in a gallery. Kahlo's house, La Casa Azul, is saturated cobalt blue on the outside, a color loud enough to be a landmark. Contributes: permission for the hero section to be big, immediate, and unapologetically saturated rather than restrained, plus the cobalt blue itself (see Palette).

**Mexico. Talavera de Puebla ceramics.** A colonial-era craft tradition still practiced by hand today: a cream ceramic ground carrying repeating cobalt, marigold-yellow, and green motifs in a tightly bounded pattern grammar. Contributes: the idea of a warm, cream (not white) neutral ground carrying a disciplined, repeating accent pattern, useful for section dividers or background texture that doesn't compete with content.

**Panama. Guna mola textiles.** Reverse-appliqué panels built from several layers of contrasting cloth, cut and stitched back to reveal the layer beneath: geometric or figurative (birds, fish, plants), always in saturated red, yellow, black, and blue. Each mola is one artisan's individual variation on a shared visual grammar. Contributes: a model for "structured personalization," a repeating system (a card grid, an icon set) where every instance is visibly hand-considered rather than a single template stamped out identically.

**Colombia and Venezuela. Wayuu mochila weaving.** Tapestry-crocheted bags made by Wayuu artisans of the Guajira Peninsula, where the geometric pattern is literally a story the weaver is telling: animals, plants, personal symbols, worked in bright, high-contrast color combinations. Contributes: the case for pattern as narrative. Decorative elements on the site (dividers, icon treatments) can be built from a consistent geometric vocabulary rather than arbitrary decoration.

**Bolivia, and the wider Andes (Peru, Ecuador). Aguayo weaving.** A woven cloth with a clear compositional structure: a bordered edge (*t'irja*), a solid-color field (*pampa*), and a patterned band (*salta*), built from magenta, red, and green wool, often naturally dyed. Contributes: a concrete, three-part composition pattern (border, field, pattern band) that maps cleanly onto how a section of a webpage is actually built. A good structural reference for how much "decoration" versus open space a section should carry.

**Brazil. Neo-Concretism (Hélio Oiticica, Lygia Clark, late 1950s to 1960s).** A Rio-based break from strict geometric Concrete art, arguing that color and form should be felt and participated in, not just looked at: Oiticica's hanging color constructions, Clark's manipulable *Bichos* sculptures. Contributes: the argument for interactivity as a form of warmth, not just a UX nicety. An element you can nudge, or that responds to you, is doing the same job Neo-Concretism assigned to participation fifty years ago.

**Cuba. Wifredo Lam, *The Jungle* (1943).** Lam fused Cubism and Surrealism with Afro-Cuban religious imagery and the visual density of Cuban sugarcane fields: crescent-shaped masks, dense vertical forms, a palette of deep greens, blues, and purples. Contributes: the deep green used as a secondary accent (see Palette), and the reminder that "Latin American art" already contains Afro-Caribbean and Indigenous visual traditions, not only the muralist mainstream. Part of why the reference set can't stop at Mexico.

## 3. Role-model / community-spotlight angle

The brief is specific here: the site needs to make LOGICA's members feel like they're standing next to something real, without inventing anything to manufacture that feeling. Two things can do this honestly.

First, Featured Content becomes the spotlight mechanism. CONTENT.md already asks for "Featured LOGICA announcements, projects, or community content" with a title, description, and link. Use that slot specifically to rotate real LOGICA people and real outcomes: a member's internship, a hackathon placement, a project shipped, a talk given, with a name, a photo, and a link back to their own profile page (already specified in CONTENT.md section 4). This is the entire mechanism: real people, real and specific accomplishments, sourced from the org itself, not written copy pretending to be a testimonial.

Second, the "shape" of ambition, held at arm's length. The brief's reference points (a Rick Owens-level aesthetic point of view, Latinx people who've reached major roles in modern life, Duolingo's willingness to be a specific, weird brand) aren't names to print on the page. But one of them is worth knowing about directly, because it's an exact, real match for the brief instead of an analogy: **Luis von Ahn**, the Guatemalan computer scientist who invented CAPTCHA and reCAPTCHA and co-founded Duolingo, is the same person who gave Duolingo the confidence to be an unmistakable, specific brand instead of a generic edtech product. He's proof the brief's own comparison point already has a Latinx computer scientist inside it. Naming him, or peers like him (Ellen Ochoa, the first Latina astronaut; Sonia Sotomayor; France Córdova, the first Latina to direct the National Science Foundation) isn't required, but it establishes the *category* of achievement the spotlight mechanism should be reaching for: specific, technical, and real, not aspirational in the abstract.

Third, no invented numbers, ever. If LOGICA doesn't have "500+ members placed at top companies" to point to honestly, the page doesn't say it. A single named, linked, real story is worth more here than any stat block, invented or not, and it's the only version of this that's actually allowed under the hard-ban list.

## 4. Palette

Every color below was pulled from a specific reference in section 2, not chosen first and justified after. Hex values for historical dye and pigment traditions (Talavera, Wayuu, aguayo) are representative: sampled against commonly reproduced photographs of the real objects, since these are handmade, natural-dye crafts without a single official spec. The two colors with a genuine documented hex, Mexican Pink and cobalt blue, are noted as such below.

| Swatch | Hex | Source | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rosa Mexicano | `#E4007C` | Named and popularized by Mexican designer Ramón Valdiosera in 1949, rooted in pre-Hispanic textile pink and bougainvillea. This is its standard documented hex. | **Primary.** The loudest color on the page, used sparingly (CTA button, key accents) |
| Cobalt Blue | `#0047AB` | The standard pigment hex for cobalt blue, the color of Talavera de Puebla glaze and Frida Kahlo's Casa Azul walls. | **Primary.** The main structural color: links, headings on light backgrounds, dark-mode surfaces |
| Cempasúchil Yellow | `#F2A900` | Sampled from the marigold (cempasúchil) accent glaze in Talavera ceramics and the yellow appliqué layer in Guna molas. | **Secondary accent.** Small, high-energy touches (icons, tags, hover states); never as text on a light background (see contrast note) |
| Jungle Green | `#1B4D3E` | Sampled from the dense greens in Wifredo Lam's *The Jungle* (1943). | **Secondary.** A second "safe" text/link color alongside cobalt, for variety in dense content |
| Ink | `#12100E` | A warm near-black, not a pure gray-black, closer to the dark outlines in Rivera's murals than to a default UI black. | **Neutral.** Primary text, dark backgrounds |
| Paper | `#F5EDE1` | A warm cream, matched to the ceramic ground of Talavera pottery rather than a cold white. | **Neutral.** Primary background |

**Primary pair:** Rosa Mexicano and Cobalt Blue, used together but not evenly. Cobalt should carry most of the page's weight (text, structure), with Rosa Mexicano reserved for the handful of moments that should feel loud (the call to action, a key headline word). Cempasúchil and Jungle Green are support, not a third and fourth equal color; the brief's own instruction ("don't use all of them at once on one screen") holds.

**Contrast, checked against both neutrals** (WCAG AA requires 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text and UI elements):

- Cobalt Blue on Paper: **7.3:1**, passes for body text.
- Cobalt Blue on white: **8.4:1**, passes for body text.
- Jungle Green on Paper: **8.3:1**, passes for body text.
- Ink on Paper: **16.4:1**, passes comfortably; this is the default body-text pairing.
- Rosa Mexicano on white: **4.6:1**, passes AA, but only just. Safe for large/bold text and buttons; do not set small body copy in Rosa Mexicano.
- Rosa Mexicano on Paper: **3.9:1**, fails AA for body text, passes for large text and UI components only. **Flag: never use Rosa Mexicano for small text on the cream background.** Reserve it for buttons, large headline words, and borders/icons where the 3:1 UI-component threshold applies.
- Cempasúchil on white or Paper: **roughly 2:1 and 1.7:1**, fails outright as a text color on either light neutral. **Flag: Cempasúchil is a fill and accent color only.** It works as a button/tag background with Ink text on top (9.4:1, passes easily), never as text or fine detail directly on Paper or white.

## 5. Type

Pair a display face with real Latin American sign-painting and lettering DNA against a clean, highly legible body face: the same "structure holds the energy" logic as the color palette. **Sudtipos**, a type foundry founded in Buenos Aires by Alejandro Paul, specializes in exactly this: typefaces drawn from Latin American commercial sign-painting, packaging lettering, and script traditions, with real technical polish (Type Directors Club recognition, available on Adobe Fonts). A bold, confident Sudtipos display face, used only for headlines and wordmark-adjacent moments, gives the page a display voice that's actually from the tradition it's referencing, instead of a generic "ethnic-coded" display font picked for vibes.

Pair it with a clean, neutral sans for everything else: body copy, UI labels, forms. The display face should never be asked to be legible at 14px; the sans face carries all of that weight. This mirrors the brief's own instruction that the contrast between bold display and clean body is the point, and decoration should never be allowed to hurt readability. Confirming a specific Sudtipos family and a specific body-face pairing, and getting both cleared for licensing and self-hosting under the performance budget in `DESIGN.md`, is a follow-up task, not something to lock in this document.

## 6. Voice and tone

LOGICA's own language, pulled directly from its UIC organization page and LinkedIn listing, is short and direct: *"Increasing the participation and success of students from Latinx and underrepresented communities pursuing careers in the field of computing and computer science,"* organized around three pillars: **diversity, growth and development, and community.** That's the voice to write toward: plain, purposeful, first-person-plural ("we," not "LOGICA offers its members"), and never vague. The landing page's hero copy should be able to answer, in one sentence, the same thing LOGICA's own mission statement answers: who this is for, and what it does for them. No copy that could be swapped onto a different org's site without editing.

## 7. Motion and interaction principles

At the level of intent, not implementation (implementation is `DESIGN.md`'s job, and it already sets the right technical bar: CSS-first, GSAP for real orchestration only, Lottie for vector moments, every animation gets a `prefers-reduced-motion` fallback):

- Motion should feel like Cruz-Diez's color interaction, not decoration: something that reveals a relationship (this leads to that, this is part of a set) rather than something that just moves because motion is available.
- A scroll-driven reveal sequence on the landing page should tell LOGICA's story in order: who we are, what we do, how you get in, the same sequencing `DESIGN.md` already calls for.
- Interactive elements (hover, focus, a nudge-able component) should carry a little of Oiticica and Clark's "art you participate in": small, purposeful responses that reward attention, not gratuitous animation on static content.
- The mark itself is a strong candidate for a single, well-made load-in moment, the branching circuit tracing itself in, Netflix-sting style, but that's a specific, narrow piece of craft worth its own follow-up spec, not something to design in the abstract here.

## 8. Mapping to CONTENT.md's landing-page sections

- **Introduction to LOGICA.** This is where voice (section 6) does the most work: state plainly who LOGICA is and who it serves, in LOGICA's own words, backed by the hero's visual confidence (bold type, a primary-color moment) rather than a generic stock-photo tech hero.
- **Organization Mission.** A natural place for the grid-literate, Torres-García-influenced structure: mission, goals, and what LOGICA provides as three or four clearly bounded blocks rather than a paragraph, echoing "logic" directly.
- **Upcoming Events.** A card-based list is a good fit for the mola/Wayuu idea of a repeating system where every instance is individually considered: a consistent event-card template with room for each event's own image or color accent.
- **Featured Content.** This is the community-spotlight mechanism described in section 3: real members, real outcomes, a name and a link, styled as the page's most "art object" moment (closer to a framed piece than a generic content card).
- **Call to Action.** The section that should carry the most Rosa Mexicano: a confident, specific invitation (not "Learn more" but something that names what joining actually gets someone), reflecting the "exclusive" half of the brief's emotional target: something worth wanting in, not just being invited to.

## 9. What this explicitly is not

Restating the hard-ban list as it applies directly to this direction, so nothing here gets reinterpreted later:

- **No pill-shaped buttons.** The grid-literate, Torres-García-influenced structure this direction calls for reads as rectangular and intentional: sharp or lightly rounded corners, not capsule shapes.
- **No vague hero copy.** Section 6 exists specifically to rule this out. The hero has to say who LOGICA is and what it does, in LOGICA's own words, not generic "empowering the next generation" copy.
- **No fake reviews or testimonials.** Section 3's entire point is that the spotlight mechanism only ever shows real, named, linked LOGICA members and real outcomes, never a written-to-sound-authentic quote.
- **No purple gradients.** The palette in section 4 is deliberately built from saturated, sourced hues (Mexican pink, cobalt, marigold, jungle green) instead of a default purple-blue gradient treatment.
- **No fake or invented metrics.** No stat block, no "500+ members," no invented percentage. If LOGICA can't back a number with a real source, it doesn't appear.
- **No AI-generated images**, and **no "made with AI" badge or tag** anywhere on the page.
- **No em dashes anywhere in copy.**
- **No emoji used as icons.** Any iconography should be drawn (or drawn from the same geometric/pattern vocabulary described in section 2), not emoji standing in for real icons.

## 10. Hero treatment — validated through visual exploration

Section 7 flagged the mark's load-in moment as "a specific, narrow piece of
craft worth its own follow-up spec." Three rounds of AI-generated landing-page
mockups, used only to feel-test the direction, produced one finding specific
enough to write down before it's lost.

**The mark works as a literal object, not an icon.** Rendered as a physical,
lit sculpture — the branching circuit tree inside its circle, built from a
blended material (metal or glass circuit lines against a warm ceramic- or
bronze-toned frame, echoing the Talavera and metalwork references in section
2, rather than pure chrome) — it reads as a centerpiece the hero can be built
around, closer to a Netflix-sting object than a flat logo lockup. This is
worth treating as a real direction for the eventual animated load-in, not
just a mockup flourish.

**The exclusive/welcoming tension in section 1 is resolved by material
choice, not by toning down either half.** The first attempt at a dark hero
used cold signifiers — stone plinths, museum lighting, engraved inscriptions
— and read as an intimidating institution rather than an org someone wants to
join. Swapping those for warm, curated-object signifiers (soft ambient
light, natural materials, a sense of a lived-in space rather than a
monument) while keeping the sculptural mark and its cobalt glow as the one
unambiguous "precision/tech" anchor produced a hero that reads as both
exclusive and inviting at once. The takeaway for whoever builds the real
hero: dark and dramatic does not have to mean cold and institutional. Avoid
literal stone, columns, or museum-plaza staging; favor warm materials and
soft light instead.

**The grid-cell pictograph idea (section 2, Torres-García) works at small
scale too.** Simple dimensional relief icons, built from the same primary
color blocks as the palette, are a strong candidate for how each Mission
sub-block or similar small content unit gets its own icon, instead of a
generic line-icon set.

Caveat, stated plainly: this was image-generation exploration for a gut-check
on direction, not asset production. Nothing rendered in these explorations is
a real photo, a real icon, or real copy — a real 3D artist or motion designer
still has to actually build the mark's load-in, and every generated face,
name, or headline in these mockups is a placeholder standing in for where
real members, real photos, and LOGICA's real voice (section 6) belong.
